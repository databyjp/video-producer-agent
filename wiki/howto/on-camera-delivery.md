# On-Camera Delivery

Natural delivery on a teleprompter is a learnable skill, not a talent. Professional production — from late-night comedy to broadcast news — has well-established techniques. The biggest gains come from the writing (see [Writing for the Ear](../writing-for-the-ear.md)), but delivery itself has its own craft.

## Rehearsal Before Recording

**Never cold-read your final take.** Familiarity with the script is what separates delivery that sounds like you from delivery that sounds like reading. NBC anchor Vicky Nguyen: *"If you can read over the scripts before, rather than read them cold on a teleprompter, that's always the best option."*

### The Three-Read Method

Before every recording session, read the script three times:

1. **Silent read** — for content and comprehension. Flag anything you don't understand or would naturally phrase differently.
2. **Aloud read** — for phrasing. Flag anything that sounds stiff, trips you up, or doesn't sound like you. Rewrite those phrases *now*, before you record.
3. **On the app at recording speed** — for calibration. Check scroll speed, eye-lead comfort, and column width. Adjust before the final take.

### Warm-Up Passes

Do two warm-up takes before your final record. The goal is not memorization — it's familiarity. A script you've read twice feels like territory you know. A script you're reading for the first time feels like discovery, and that shows in delivery.

**If a phrase trips you up three times in warm-up, rewrite it.** Speed is not a fix for awkward phrasing.

## Teleprompter Setup

Getting setup right is the fastest way to fix visible eye movement and rushed or hesitant pacing — before delivery technique enters the picture at all.

**Screen placement.** Position the display as close to the camera lens as physically possible. The gap between screen and lens directly determines how much your eye movement is visible on camera. Minimize it. For a tablet used as a notes display, mount it directly below or beside the lens — not off to the side.

**Column width.** Narrow text columns mean your eyes travel vertically rather than horizontally. Less lateral eye travel = less visible eye movement on camera. Pair with font size ≥ 36pt and 1.5× line spacing.

**Scroll speed.** Most people speak naturally at 125–150 WPM. Start at the low end. Technical or heavy content often lands better slower; fast-paced creator content can go up to 160 WPM. Measure your actual rate before your first session — don't guess. Adjust through warm-up passes.

**Voice-controlled scrolling.** Apps like Teleprompter.com offer speech-recognition scrolling — the text moves with your voice. This removes the mismatch between a fixed scroll speed and natural delivery variation. Worth using if available.

**Notes mode as an alternative.** If word-for-word prompting feels constraining, use the teleprompter as an outline display — key points and transitions rather than full sentences. You speak more freely; it sounds less like recitation. More useful for experienced speakers who've internalized the structure and can speak fluently from a prompt.

## The Eye-Lead Technique

This is the single most important teleprompter skill. Most first-time users read the word they're currently saying. Experienced users read the word that's coming next.

**How it works.** While your voice delivers the current phrase, your eyes have already scanned the next line. That half-second of preview gives your brain time to process the upcoming phrasing — so when you speak it, you deliver it with intention (adjusting emphasis, pace, and tone) rather than reacting to it as it appears on screen.

**How to build it.** Set the scroll slightly faster than comfortable. The mild pressure forces your eyes to read ahead. Once the habit feels automatic at that speed, return to your natural pace — the eye-lead carries over.

**Timeline.** Usually clicks after 3–4 focused practice sessions.

## Delivery: What to Control

### Vary Pace, Pitch, and Emphasis

Monotone is the #1 tell that someone is reading. When every sentence arrives at the same speed and pitch, the content sounds recited even when the words are excellent. The fix is to build variation into the script before you record — not to try to fake it in the moment.

Three things to vary deliberately:
- **Pace** — faster for energy, slower for weight or gravity
- **Pitch** — the natural rise and fall of conversational speech
- **Emphasis** — which words you lean into

Mark all three in your script using underline, bold, CAPS, ellipses (...), or `[PAUSE]` cues (see [Writing for the Ear — Formatting for Delivery](../writing-for-the-ear.md#formatting-for-delivery)). You can't reliably improvise all three under the pressure of recording; built-in cues are more reliable than memory.

### Don't Stop at Periods

NBC anchor Laura Jarrett: *"I remember the first executive producer told me, 'Don't stop.' If you see a period, don't just pause as if you were reading it. Just keep going. From then on I never use periods."*

Periods in a script are breathing cues, not stop signs. The voice should carry momentum through sentence breaks. Pausing hard at every period is one of the clearest markers of someone reading rather than speaking.

### Slow Down More Than You Think You Need To

Jonathan Gonzalez, NBC4: *"I tend to race through my script, so I have to constantly tell myself slow down. And when I watch the final product, it sounds just fine."*

Slower delivery gives you time to adjust your tone to the content's emotional register — serious, energetic, dry, warm. If you're rushing, you can't do any of that. Reading slowly also sounds intentional rather than hurried, which reads as authority.

### Treat It as a Conversation

Morgan Radford, NBC News: *"You can use teleprompter to have a casual conversation."*

The instinct to adopt a "presenter voice" — more formal, more projected, more performative — is one of the biggest barriers to natural delivery. You're talking to one person watching a video on their laptop. Not addressing a room.

### Edit Into Your Own Voice

Even if you wrote the draft yourself, before recording, read every phrase and ask: *would I actually say this?* If not, rewrite it. The words should feel like yours because they essentially are — by the time you're recording, you shouldn't be encountering any phrase for the first time.

NBC anchor Emilie Ikeda: *"Don't be afraid to make edits, to put something into your own voice. And always remember to refine your writing throughout your entire career."*

## The Muted Playback Review

After a practice take, mute the audio and watch only the video.

What you'll see that audio playback misses:
- Micro-expressions when you hit a difficult phrase
- Brow tension when the scroll gets ahead of you
- The exact moments where your eyes visibly scan instead of holding the lens
- Body language that contradicts the emotional register of what you're saying

Fix what you see before recording the final take. This takes two minutes and catches problems that are invisible while you're recording.

## Short-Segment Recording

You don't have to deliver a 10-minute video in one take. Record in short segments and assemble in editing — this is exactly what professional pre-taped shows do.

**Benefits:**
- Removes the psychological pressure of a flawless long take
- Lets you concentrate fully on one section at a time
- Errors in segment 3 don't invalidate segments 1 and 2
- Post-production can even dub a single wrong word if needed (confirmed practice at LWT)

This is not a workaround for imperfect delivery. It's the professional approach for pre-taped content.

## Quick Reference

| Problem | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Sounds robotic | Rewrite script for the ear — see [Writing for the Ear](../writing-for-the-ear.md) |
| Eyes visibly scanning | Move screen closer to lens; narrow column width; increase font size |
| Rushing or stumbling | Slow down; calibrate scroll speed; rewrite the phrase |
| Monotone delivery | Mark emphasis and pauses in script; vary pace deliberately |
| Losing your place | Practice the eye-lead technique |
| Long-take pressure | Record in short segments; assemble in edit |
| Presenter voice | You're talking to one person — have a conversation |

## Long-Term: What Actually Builds Presence

The naturalness of hosts like John Oliver and Stephen Colbert on teleprompter is inseparable from their backgrounds: Oliver in UK stand-up comedy, Colbert in improv at Second City. Both built the core muscle that makes scripted delivery look unscripted: **delivering material with authentic intention, not recitation**.

For a developer advocate, the path is less extreme but the principle is the same:
- Giving the same talk live at a conference or meetup before recording it helps internalize structure and timing
- Presenting technical content to real humans — even informally — builds the habit of speaking *to* someone rather than at a camera
- Improv and stand-up training directly build delivery-under-pressure skills

The teleprompter techniques above are learnable in weeks. Genuine on-camera presence that holds across a 15-minute technical explainer takes months of consistent practice. The compound effect is real.
